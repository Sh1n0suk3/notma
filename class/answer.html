Answers for Quiz#1 Voltage

Science/Lab

Questions 10

Question 1

The difference in electrical potential energy between two places is called?

voltage

Think of it like the height difference of a waterfall. This potential gives the water the ability to flow. Voltage is the "electrical height difference."

Question 2

What 'flows' in a wire?

Electrons

In metal conductors like wires, the electric current is the organized movement of negatively charged electrons.

Question 3

What is voltage?

What pushes the free electrons around a circuit

If you imagine electrons as water in a pipe, voltage is the "pressure" from a pump that pushes the water along. Without voltage, the electrons would not move.

Question 4

Define the potential difference?

How much work can potentially be done by a circuit

A greater potential difference (voltage) allows more work to be done per unit of charge, resulting in a higher power output from the circuit. This relationship is described by the power formula \( P = \frac{V^2}{R} \).

Question 5

How do aligned batteries affect voltage?

More volts means more power

Aligning batteries in series (positive to negative) makes their voltages add up. This higher total voltage can deliver more power, as described by the formula \( P = \frac{V^2}{R} \).

Question 6

Side by side batteries does what?

Lengthen the possible usage without increasing voltage.

Placing batteries in parallel (positive to positive) increases the total capacity (Amp-hours). The voltage "push" does not get stronger, but it can last much longer.

Question 7

Where does the term Volt come from?

Alessandro Volta

The unit "Volt" is named in honor of Alessandro Volta, the Italian physicist who invented the first electrical battery.

Question 8

What is the difference between volts and voltage?

Voltage is the pressure and Volts are the units to measure that pressure

"Voltage" is the concept, like "length." "Volts" are the units you measure it in, like "meters."

Question 9

What is the socket voltage in Thailand?

220V

This is the standard residential voltage set by the electrical grid in Thailand and many other parts of the world.

Question 10

What is the voltage of US wall sockets?

110V

While the modern standard is technically \( 120 \, \mathrm{V} \), it is commonly referred to as \( 110 \, \mathrm{V} \) in the United States.

Answers for CW#1 Current

Science/Lab

Questions 10

Question 1

A closed path through which electrons can flow is ___.

a circuit

For electricity to flow continuously, it needs a complete, unbroken loop from the power source and back again. This closed path is called a circuit.

Question 2

The flow of electrons through a wire or any conductor is called___.

current

It is a measurement of how much electric charge is flowing past a point per second.

Question 3

Current is measured in ___.

amps

The standard unit for measuring electric current is the Ampere, usually shortened to "Amps" (\( \mathrm{A} \)).

Question 4

Electrons flow from areas of ___ potential energy to areas of ___ potential energy.

higher, lower

Like a ball rolling downhill, electrons naturally move from a state of high energy (the negative terminal) to a state of low energy (the positive terminal).

Question 5

If the Voltage is increased and the resistance stays the same, Current will?

increase

According to Ohm's Law, \(I = \frac{V}{R}\), current (\( \mathrm{I} \)) is directly proportional to voltage (\( \mathrm{V} \)). Therefore, increasing the voltage while resistance stays the same will cause the current to increase.

Question 6

If the Voltage stays the same and Resistance is increased, Current will?

decrease

According to Ohm's Law, \(I = \frac{V}{R}\), current (\( \mathrm{I} \)) is inversely proportional to resistance (\( \mathrm{R} \)). Therefore, increasing the resistance while voltage stays the same will cause the current to decrease.

Question 7

In a series circuit, which of the following is the same throughout the circuit?

Current

A series circuit is like a single-lane road. The number of cars passing any point per minute must be the same everywhere. The current is constant.

Question 8

What is current measured in?

A (Amps)

The unit is the Ampere, and its official symbol in formulas and measurements is \( \mathrm{A} \).

Question 9

What is the unit of power?

Watts

Electrical power, which is the rate at which energy is used, is measured in Watts (\( \mathrm{W} \)).

Question 10

Which part of a circuit starts and stops the flow of electrical current?

switch

A switch works by creating a deliberate break (opening) in the circuit to stop the flow or completing the path (closing) to allow it to flow.

Answers for HW#1 Resistance

Science/Lab

Questions 10

Question 1

If a circuit has two resistors in series both are 4Ω, what is the total resistance of the circuit?

8Ω

For resistors in series, the total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances. The formula is \(R_{total} = R_1 + R_2\), so the calculation is \(4\Omega + 4\Omega = 8\Omega\).

Question 2

If a circuit has two resistors in parallel both are 4Ω, what is the total resistance of the circuit?

2Ω

For two identical resistors in parallel, the total resistance is half the individual resistance. The general formula, \(\frac{1}{R_{total}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\), confirms that the total is \(2\Omega\).

Question 3

What effect do electrons flowing through a resistor have on the resistor?

heat it up

The "friction" of electrons pushing through a resistive material converts electrical energy into heat. This is how toasters and electric heaters work.

Question 4

As the length of a wire increases, the resistance of the wire will?

increase

The longer the path, the more opposition the electrons face. Resistance is directly proportional to length.

Question 5

As the diameter of a wire increases, the resistance of the wire will?

decrease

A thicker wire is like a wider highway; it allows more current to flow easily. Resistance decreases as the wire's area increases.

Question 6

What is resistance measured in?

Ω (Ohms)

The standard unit for electrical resistance is the Ohm, represented by the Greek letter Omega (\(\Omega \)).

Question 7

What is Resistance?

The ability to reduce the flow of electrons

This is a great functional definition. Resistance is the measure of a material's opposition to the flow of electric current.

Question 8

The mathematical relationship among Voltage, Current, and Resistance?

Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law is the fundamental principle connecting these three quantities, defined by the formula: \(V = I \cdot R\).

Question 9

Voltage divided by Current? (V / I )

Resistance

This is a direct rearrangement of Ohm's Law. From \(V = I \cdot R\), dividing by current (I) gives you the formula for resistance: \(R = \frac{V}{I}\).

Question 10

The resistance of a wires depends on ___.

the metal it is made from

The resistance of a wire depends on four things: its material, length, thickness, and temperature.

Answers for HW#2 Watts & Power

Science/Lab

Questions 10

Question 1

What is a watt?

A unit of power

A watt \( \mathrm{W} \) is the standard unit of power, representing the rate at which energy is transferred or used. It is formally defined as one joule of energy per second.

Question 2

How is power in watts calculated using voltage and current?

Power (W) = Voltage (V) × Current (A)

This fundamental relationship is known as Watt's Law. The formula is \(P = V \cdot I\), where power \( \mathrm{P} \) in watts is the product of voltage \( \mathrm{V} \) and current \( \mathrm{I} \).

Question 3

In which scenario would you be using watts to measure power?

Calculating the energy needed to power a lightbulb

Power, measured in watts, quantifies the rate at which an electrical device, such as a lightbulb, consumes energy.

Question 4

If a device operates at 10 volts and 2 amperes, what is its power consumption in watts?

20 watts

Using the power formula \(P = V \cdot I\), the calculation is \(10V \cdot 2A = 20W\).

Question 5

What is the practical application of measuring power in watts for household appliances?

To understand the energy use of the appliance

The wattage of an appliance indicates its rate of electricity consumption. This information is crucial for estimating how much energy it will use over time, which directly affects electricity costs.

Question 6

If a light bulb uses 60 watts of power and operates for 2 hours, how much energy does it consume?

120 watt-hours

Energy consumption is found by multiplying power by time. Using the formula \(E = P \cdot t\), the calculation is \(60W \cdot 2h = 120Wh\).

Question 7

How does understanding watts help in reducing electricity bills?

By choosing appliances that require lower power to use

Since electricity bills are based on total energy consumption (often in kilowatt-hours), selecting appliances with lower wattage ratings for the same task means they use less energy over time, leading to lower bills.

Question 8

If a device operates at 5 volts and 3 amperes, what is its power consumption in watts?

15 watts

Using the power formula \(P = V \cdot I\), the calculation is \(5V \cdot 3A = 15W\).

Question 9

What is the significance of a device having a high wattage?

It uses more energy

A higher wattage rating signifies that a device consumes energy at a faster rate. Over the same period, a high-wattage device will use more total energy than a low-wattage device.

Question 10

If a heater uses 1500 watts of power and operates for 3 hours, how much energy does it consume?

4500 watt-hours

Energy consumption is the product of power and time (\(E = P \cdot t\)). The calculation is \(1500W \cdot 3h = 4500Wh\), which is also equal to 4.5 kilowatt-hours (kWh).

Answers for Quiz#1 Pancreas

Supplemental Science

Questions 5

Question 1

Diabetes Type I

Insulin is not produced

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disorder where the body's immune system destroys the insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas, resulting in a complete lack of insulin.

Question 2

Hormone that causes blood sugar to be lowered?

Insulin

Insulin acts like a key, signaling cells throughout the body to absorb glucose from the bloodstream to be used for energy, which effectively lowers blood sugar levels.

Question 3

Hormone that causes blood sugar to be raised?

Glucagon

When blood sugar is low, glucagon is the hormone that signals the liver to break down its stored form of glucose (glycogen) and release it into the bloodstream.

Question 4

Monosaccharide that is found in blood that cells can use for energy?

Glucose

Glucose is the body's primary fuel. It is a simple sugar (monosaccharide) that circulates in the blood and is used by cells to produce energy through cellular respiration.

Question 5

Gland that secretes hormones that regulate blood glucose?

Pancreas

The pancreas is the primary gland for blood sugar control, as it produces and secretes both insulin (to lower blood sugar) and glucagon (to raise blood sugar) from specialized cells.

Answers for CW#1 Adrenal

Supplemental Science

Questions 5

Question 1

What does the adrenal gland look like?

Small, triangular gland, divided into regions.

Each adrenal gland is a small, triangular-shaped organ that sits on top of a kidney. It is divided into two distinct parts: the outer adrenal cortex and the inner adrenal medulla, each producing different hormones.

Question 2

Differences between Epinephrine and Norepinephrine?

Epinephrine leads to greater cardiac output.

While both are 'fight-or-flight' hormones, epinephrine (adrenaline) more strongly stimulates the heart's beta receptors, leading to more forceful contractions and a greater increase in cardiac output than norepinephrine.

Question 3

Which of the following about cortisol is not true?

secretion is elevated during sleep

Cortisol levels follow a daily rhythm. They are at their lowest during the first few hours of sleep and peak shortly after waking up to promote alertness. Therefore, the statement that its secretion is elevated during sleep is not true.

Question 4

The medulla releases which of the two hormones when stimulated by the sympathetic nervous system?

Noradrenaline, Adrenaline

The adrenal medulla is directly linked to the sympathetic nervous system. When the 'fight-or-flight' response is triggered, the medulla releases the hormones adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine).

Question 5

Where are the adrenal glands located?

above the kidneys

The adrenal glands are part of the endocrine system and are located on the top of each kidney in the retroperitoneal space. Their name even means "near the kidney."

Regulate sleep-wake cycles

Melatonin is best known as the 'hormone of darkness.' Its primary function is to signal to the body that it is nighttime, helping to regulate the timing of sleep and wakefulness (the circadian rhythm).

Question 5

Which type of light has the most powerful effect on the sleep-wake internal body clock?

Blue light

Specialized cells in the eye's retina that regulate our internal clock are most sensitive to light in the blue wavelength range. Exposure to this type of light, common from screens, is the most powerful signal for inhibiting melatonin.

Question 6

Circadian rhythms are natural processes that regulate ___.

Sleep wake cycles

Circadian rhythms are the 24-hour internal clocks that regulate many of our body's essential functions and processes. The most important and well-known circadian rhythm is the sleep-wake cycle.

Question 7

What happens to melatonin levels during the day?

They decrease

Due to the strong inhibitory effect of daylight, the body's natural production of melatonin is very low during the daytime. Levels only begin to rise as darkness falls in the evening.

Question 8

How does darkness affect melatonin production?

It increases melatonin production

In the absence of light, the brain's internal clock (the SCN) signals the pineal gland to begin producing and secreting melatonin, which helps induce sleepiness and prepare the body for rest.

Question 9

What is a malfunction of the pineal gland? How would this affect you?

Less melatonin secretion. It would affect your sleeping patterns and may cause you to suffer from insomnia.

A malfunctioning pineal gland may produce insufficient melatonin. This disrupts the body's internal clock and its ability to time sleep correctly, which can lead to sleep pattern disorders like insomnia.

Question 10

What is the possible side effect of taking too much melatonin?

Drowsiness

The most common side effect of taking too much melatonin is next-day drowsiness or grogginess, as the hormone's sleep-inducing effects have not fully worn off. It can also cause dizziness or headaches.

Answers for Quiz#2 Growth Hormone

Supplemental Science

Questions 10

Question 1

What is the main role of growth hormone in the body?

Helping the body grow and develop

Growth Hormone (GH) is a key anabolic hormone that stimulates cell reproduction, regeneration, and overall growth, particularly during childhood and adolescence.

Question 2

When is the growth hormone most active?

During sleep

Growth Hormone is released in pulses, but the largest and most significant releases occur at night during the deep stages of slow-wave sleep, which is why quality sleep is vital for development.

Question 3

What happens if a child has too little growth hormone?

They will be short

A deficiency of Growth Hormone during childhood impairs the normal rate of development, leading to a condition known as pituitary dwarfism, which results in significantly shorter adult stature.

Question 4

What can cause the body to produce more growth hormone naturally?

Sleeping well and exercising

The body's natural release of Growth Hormone is powerfully stimulated by physiological states such as deep sleep, high-intensity exercise, and fasting.

Question 5

Which gland controls the production of many hormones, including growth hormone?

Pituitary

The pituitary gland, a small pea-sized gland at the base of the brain, is often called the 'master gland' because it produces and secretes Growth Hormone along with many other critical hormones that control body functions.

Question 6

Growth hormone mainly affects which part of the body?

Bones and muscles

While Growth Hormone affects nearly all tissues in the body, its most noticeable effects are on the musculoskeletal system, where it promotes the linear growth of long bones and an increase in muscle mass.

Question 7

What is the name of the condition caused by too much growth hormone?

Gigantism

An overproduction of Growth Hormone in childhood, before the bone growth plates have fused, leads to a condition called gigantism, characterized by excessive and rapid growth resulting in exceptional height.

Question 8

What is signal transduction?

The series of steps by which a cell responds to a signal

Signal transduction is the fundamental process by which a cell converts an external signal (like a hormone) into a functional response. It's the 'cause-and-effect' pathway inside a cell.

Question 9

Where does signal transduction typically begin?

At the cell membrane

For most protein-based hormones that cannot enter a cell, the signal transduction pathway must begin at the cell's boundary. The hormone binds to a specific receptor on the cell membrane, initiating the internal response.

Question 10

What is the role of a cell receptor protein in signal transduction?

To bind to the hormone and initiate a response

The cell receptor protein acts as a specific 'docking station' for the hormone. When the hormone binds, the receptor changes shape, an action that triggers the first step in the chain reaction of the internal signaling pathway.

Answers for Quiz#1 Female Reproductive System

Health

Questions 10

Question 1

Which hormone causes the egg to ripen in the ovary?

FSH

Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH), released by the pituitary gland, directly stimulates the growth and maturation of follicles within the ovaries. Each follicle contains a developing egg.

Question 2

Which hormone spikes and consequently causes the release of the egg into the uterus?

LH

A sharp spike in Luteinizing Hormone (LH) from the pituitary gland is the direct trigger for ovulation—the moment the mature egg is released from the ovary into the fallopian tube.

Ma note:The egg is technically released into the fallopian tube, not directly into the uterus.

Question 3

What two hormones does the pituitary gland produce?

FSH & LH

In the context of reproduction, the anterior pituitary gland produces two critical hormones that act on the ovaries: Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) and Luteinizing Hormone (LH).

Question 4

What is responsible for creating the hormones estrogen & progesterone?

The ovaries

The ovaries are the primary production site for female sex hormones. Estrogen is produced by the developing follicle, and after ovulation, the remnant of the follicle (the corpus luteum) produces progesterone.

Question 5

What hormone(s) is responsible for thickening the lining of the uterus?

estrogen

In the first half of the menstrual cycle, rising levels of estrogen are responsible for rebuilding the uterine lining (endometrium) and causing it to thicken in preparation for a potential pregnancy.

Question 6

What is a hormone?

A Chemical messenger

Hormones are chemical messengers produced by endocrine glands. They travel through the bloodstream to target tissues and organs to regulate most of the body's major systems.

Question 7

Follicle Stimulating Hormones function is to?

mature eggs in the ovaries

The primary function of Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) is to stimulate the development of ovarian follicles. Typically, one of these follicles will become dominant and mature fully each cycle.

Question 8

Luteinizing Hormones main role is to?

release the egg cell

The main role of the sharp surge in Luteinizing Hormone (LH) is to cause the mature ovarian follicle to rupture and release its egg, a process known as ovulation.

Question 9

The two hormones that maintain the lining of the uterus are?

Progesterone and estrogen

After ovulation, both estrogen and progesterone work together to maintain the thickened, nutrient-rich uterine lining. Progesterone is especially critical for making the lining stable and receptive to implantation.

Question 10

What is the role of FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone)?

Causes an egg to mature in an ovary. Stimulates the ovaries to release estrogen

FSH has two key interconnected roles: it causes an egg to mature within an ovarian follicle, and it simultaneously stimulates that growing follicle to produce and release estrogen.

Question 1

In the diagram representing the menstrual cycle shown, when does ovulation take place?

between day 12 and 16

In a typical 28-day menstrual cycle, a surge of Luteinizing Hormone (LH) triggers ovulation around day 14. Therefore, the period between days 12 and 16 is the most common time for the egg to be released.

Question 2

The diagram shows the female reproductive system. The part labelled B is called?

the ovary

The ovaries are the primary female reproductive organs. They are responsible for producing, storing, and releasing eggs (ova) and also secrete the key hormones estrogen and progesterone.

Question 3

The diagram shows the female reproductive system. The part labelled A is called?

the womb

The womb, more formally known as the uterus, is the muscular organ where a fertilized egg implants. It provides a safe and nourishing environment for a fetus to develop during pregnancy.

Question 4

The part of the female reproductive system where fertilization takes place?

Fallopian Tubes

After an egg is released from the ovary, it travels into the fallopian tube. It is within this tube that fertilization—the fusion of the egg and sperm—most commonly occurs.

Question 5

Identify part 3

uterus

Assuming part 3 is the uterus, its main function is to accept a fertilized egg and provide the site for the fetus to grow and develop until birth.

Question 6

The small hair-like projection found inside the Fallopian tubes are called what?

cilia

The inner surface of the fallopian tubes is lined with cilia, which are microscopic, hair-like structures that move in a coordinated, wave-like motion.

Question 7

What is the purpose of cilia?

guide the egg through the Fallopian tube into the uterus

The constant, gentle sweeping motion of the cilia creates a current that actively guides the egg from the ovary end of the fallopian tube down towards the uterus.

Question 8

What is the function of the uterus?

Place for a baby to grow

The primary function of the uterus is to nurture a developing fetus. It is the place where a fertilized egg implants and a baby grows for nine months.

Question 9

Structure 2 is the ___?

cervix

Assuming structure 2 is the cervix, it is the lower, narrow part of the uterus that connects to the vagina. It acts as a gateway, opening and closing at different times in the cycle.

Question 10

How frequently do ovaries release an egg?

Once a month.

On average, the process of the menstrual cycle—including follicular development and ovulation—results in the release of one mature egg from an ovary approximately once every month.

Answers for HW#1 Male Reproductive System

Health

Questions 10

Question 1

What is the function of the organ shown in the picture?

used to cool down the temperature in the testes

Assuming the organ shown is the scrotum, its primary function is thermoregulation. It holds the testes outside the main body cavity to maintain a temperature slightly lower than core body temperature, which is essential for healthy sperm production.

Question 2

The male reproductive organs that produce sperm and testosterone?

Testicles

The testicles (or testes) are the primary male gonads. They are responsible for the dual functions of producing sperm cells (spermatogenesis) and secreting testosterone, the principal male sex hormone.

Question 3

The skin covered sac that surround the testes?

Scrotum

The scrotum is the sac of skin and muscle that contains the testicles. In addition to protecting them, its main role is to regulate their temperature, keeping them cool enough for sperm production.

Question 4

Once sperm are made they move into this coiled tube?

Epididymis

The epididymis is a long, tightly coiled tube resting on the back of each testicle. Immature sperm, produced in the testes, travel to the epididymis to mature and be stored.

Question 5

About how many sperm are ejaculated under normal circumstances?

Millions

A normal, healthy ejaculate is highly concentrated with sperm, typically containing anywhere from 40 million to over 300 million sperm cells to maximize the chance of fertilization.

Question 6

The process of making new sperm is called?

spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis is the specific biological term for the complex process of producing mature sperm from precursor cells within the testes.

Question 7

Sperm are formed in the ___?

seminiferous tubules

The seminiferous tubules are the network of tiny tubes located within the testes. They are the precise anatomical location where the process of spermatogenesis occurs.

Question 8

The two male reproductive organs that produce most of the fluids that make up semen are called?

Seminal Vesicle & Prostate

While the testes produce the sperm, the fluid portion of semen is largely produced by two accessory glands: the seminal vesicles (providing fructose for energy) and the prostate gland (providing a protective, enzymatic fluid).

Question 9

The primary male hormone is ___?

testosterone

Testosterone is the primary male sex hormone. It is essential for the development of male reproductive tissues and secondary sexual characteristics like increased muscle mass, bone density, and body hair growth.

Question 10

The duct that propels live sperm toward ejaculation is the ___?

vas deferens

The vas deferens is the long, muscular duct that carries mature sperm from the epididymis up into the body cavity, where it eventually meets the ejaculatory duct in preparation for ejaculation.
